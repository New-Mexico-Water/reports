# Why Elephant Butte was low from 1951 to about 1979

Third version, 2026-10-11: the second version (`report.md`) revised after an adversarial edit; the editor's notes are in
[report.markup.md](report.markup.md). Not yet checked by a reviewer; nothing here is published. Per-year numbers from the nmwater archive
are in [elephant-butte.csv](elephant-butte.csv), rebuilt by `scripts/elephant_butte.py`; dated events in [events.yaml](events.yaml) (not yet
updated for this version); full citations in [references.md](references.md). Numbers in brackets are the sources at the end. Thousands of
acre-feet a year unless stated; water years run October to September.

## The answer

The lake was low for almost thirty years for three reasons. It went into the 1950s already empty. The river then brought it too little
to refill. And most of that shortfall came not from the sky but from what happened to the water between the mountains and the lake.

1. **The lake was emptied before the drought peaked.** It filled in 1942. For the next eight years the Rio Grande Project went on
   delivering practically normal supplies, although inflow was less than half the average in four of those years. By 1951 nearly all
   the carryover was gone [13]. In the archive, releases in 1943-1950 averaged 789,000 acre-feet a year against inflow of 669,000.
   Storage fell from 1,931,000 at the end of water year 1942 to 333,000 in 1950 and 19,000 in 1951. The same thing had happened before.
   From 1927 to 1940 the lake released 89% of its inflow and lost more to evaporation. It sank from 1,281,000 to 430,000, and only
   the floods of 1941-1942 refilled it.

2. **The climate of the low era was close to this river's long-run normal; the years it is usually compared with were not.** A tree-ring
   reconstruction of the Rio Grande near Del Norte, 1508-2002, puts 1957-1978 at 623,000 acre-feet a year. That is about 3% below the
   500-year mean of 644,000. Against that same mean, 1905-1930 ran 14% above (737,000), the wettest stretch in the record [15, 16]. USGS
   called it "a wetter quarter of a century" [13]. Against the 1930s, the headwaters were the same. On the 12 years of 1927-1940 when
   every gauge ran, Del Norte and the Conejos near Mogote carried 795,000 against 783,000 in 1957-1978. Winter precipitation over
   Colorado's headwaters was 6.9 inches against 7.1. Against the snowy 1940s and the very wet 1920s there was less snow:
   - Snow courses show April 1 snowpack 5-15% below 1941-1950 at most sites (Wolf Creek Pass 26.8 inches against 31.3) [30].
   - Winter precipitation was 7.1 inches against 8.7 in the 1940s and 8.6 in 1916-1926.

3. **Less of the mountains' water reached Otowi.** With the same headwater flow, Otowi ran 167,000 acre-feet a year lower than in the
   1930s (matched years; table below). Three pieces account for all of it:
   - **Colorado kept more.** The water kept in the San Luis Valley (the headwaters minus the flow at the state line) changed by period:
     - 489,000 a year in 1928-1940;
     - 568,000 in 1957-1967;
     - 486,000 in 1968-1978, after a court stipulation.

     The Compact Commission's accounts tell the same story. Colorado was 900 acre-feet in credit at the end of 1951. Its debit was
     153,300 by the end of 1952 and 944,400 by 1967, against a cap of 100,000 [4, 23]. The court record does not say why. The suit's
     complaint names no cause. Colorado answered that drought and the "physical deterioration" of the river were to blame [23]. A
     Colorado engineer later wrote that the 1952 shortfall's causes were largely unknown and that before 1968 Colorado administered its
     water rights without curtailing them to meet the Compact [3]. From 1968 Colorado undertook to deliver every year, curtailing
     diversions if necessary, and it did so through 1984 [3, 23].
   - **The Rio Chama carried 73,000 less (401,000 to 328,000) and its cause is not established.** Winter precipitation over New
     Mexico's northern mountains was the same in both eras (5.0 inches). The low-era figure even includes imported San Juan-Chama
     water, about 672,000 acre-feet through Azotea Tunnel in 1971-1978 [28, 29], so the native decline was larger. Storage in El Vado
     cannot explain it while the lake was low, because the Compact barred storing native water upstream while Project storage was
     under 400,000 acre-feet [12, 20].
   - **Northern New Mexico's tributaries above Embudo gave 24,000 less.**

   The Compact's schedules let both upstream states use a larger share of a smaller river. Colorado's Lobatos obligation is the sum of
   its Conejos and Del Norte schedules less 10,000 acre-feet, and both schedules fall faster than supply. New Mexico may consume a
   larger share of a small Otowi flow than of a large one [11, 20]. A dry run of years is therefore passed downstream amplified, at
   both state lines.

4. **The middle valley took a little more.** Otowi minus the inflow to the lake at San Marcial was 185,000 a year in 1927-1940 and
   219,000 in 1957-1978. That figure is net: the Jemez, Rio Puerco and Rio Salado added about 85,000 a year in 1957-1978 (archive). The
   gross loss was therefore roughly 300,000, in line with Reclamation's 1947 finding that riparian vegetation, wet sands and the river
   lost more than 300,000 acre-feet a year [11]. Things pushing the loss up:
   - Salt cedar spread "significantly" between 1935 and 1947, and by about half again between Bernardo and San Marcial in 1947-1955
     [27].
   - Albuquerque's wells were taking about 13,000 acre-feet a year from the river by 1960 and 38,000 by 1979 [26].
   - The Rio Puerco fell from 40,000 a year in the 1940s to 30,000 (archive; no record before 1940).

   Reclamation's channel works and the Low-Flow Conveyance Channel won back an estimated 40,000-71,000 a year [2, 12]. Without them
   the loss would have grown more.

   Together, against the 1930s: inflow to the lake fell from 881,000 to 628,000 a year. Of that, 219,000 was less water at Otowi and
   34,000 more lost in the valley (14 matched years).

5. **The Project lived on wells instead of storage.** Releases gave at least 3.0 acre-feet per acre every year from 1930 to 1939. In
   1951-1978 they gave the full 3.0 in only 10 of 28 years [24]. Groundwater pumping in the Elephant Butte Irrigation District rose from
   under 5,500 acre-feet in 1949 to about 290,000 in 1956 [24]. USGS described the farmers' wells as "a new reservoir" [13]. Mexico's
   share under the 1906 Convention is cut in the same proportion in drought: 43,000 acre-feet in 1951 and 49,700 in 1952, against
   60,000 [22]. With inflow of 628,000 a year, evaporation, and releases of 556,000 (89% of inflow, as in the 1930s), the lake could
   only hover.

6. **It ended with the wettest two decades since the 1920s, Colorado's full deliveries and a spill declared on paper.** In 1979-1999:
   - winter precipitation over the headwaters averaged 8.6 inches;
   - the headwaters carried 946,000 a year and Otowi 1,276,000;
   - the 1980s were the highest-flow decade of a reconstruction of the river back to 1571 [17].

   Colorado had delivered in full since 1968. On 13 June 1985 the Compact Commission declared that Elephant Butte had spilled, by
   agreement of the Commissioners. It counted flood water held in New Mexico's upstream reservoirs, about 313,600 acre-feet, to avoid an
   uncontrolled spill. Colorado's State Engineer called it a spill "on paper". Only about 7,800 acre-feet went over in July [4, 21]. The
   spill cancelled Colorado's debit of about 605,000 acre-feet and New Mexico's of about 115,000 [21]. The suit against Colorado was
   dismissed with prejudice in December 1985 [21, 23].

**Today** the snow is back near the low era's level: 7.3 inches of winter precipitation over Colorado's headwaters since 2000. Spring is
about 1 °C warmer (6.7 °C against 5.3-5.8). Reconstructions find the upper Rio Grande's runoff per unit of precipitation has fallen since
the 1980s more than at any time since 1571, and warmth makes very low-runoff years 2.5-3 times as likely in dry years [17]. Inflow since
2000 has averaged 492,000 acre-feet, below the low era. The middle valley's net loss is 308,000; the conveyance channel has been out of
service since 1985 [2]. On 29 July 2026 the lake held 26,732 acre-feet, its lowest daily reading since October 1956. Storage then and now
comes from different capacity tables (see Method).

## The Compact

- **Colorado** must deliver at Lobatos the sum of its Conejos and Del Norte schedule values less 10,000 acre-feet [20].
- **New Mexico**, since a 1948 resolution of the Commission, must deliver the Elephant Butte effective index supply: release plus
  change in storage. Lake evaporation is charged to New Mexico [11, 20].
- **Debit caps:** Colorado 100,000 acre-feet, New Mexico 200,000 [20].
- **Article VII:** while Project storage holds less than 400,000 acre-feet of usable water, neither state may add storage in reservoirs
  built after 1929 [20].
- **Actual spill:** all accrued debits are cancelled in a year of actual spill [20].
- **"Normal release"** of 790,000 acre-feet is an accounting benchmark, not an obligation [20]; the Commission booked an "under-release"
  of 362,300 acre-feet at the end of 1951 [4].

Year-end accrued debits (credit negative), acre-feet. Colorado 1952-1958 from the table in the 1966 complaint [23], whose later values
match the Commission's reports. Other years from the Commission's reports [4]: each year links to its report in
[references.md](references.md). Reports for 1952-1958 are not online, and New Mexico's balances for those years are not known.

Water Colorado owed and did not deliver at the state line could not reach the lake, though some of it would have been used or lost in
New Mexico on the way. New Mexico's debit is measured at the lake. In 1951, with the lake under 400,000 acre-feet, upstream storage was
increased against Article VII, as the Commission recorded [4].

**The suits:**
- **Texas v. New Mexico (No. 9 Original).** Texas filed in 1951 against New Mexico and the Middle Rio Grande Conservancy District. It
  asked the Court to stop storage in El Vado and other reservoirs until New Mexico's debit was back under 200,000 acre-feet. The Court
  dismissed the case in 1957, because the United States, trustee for the Pueblos' prior rights, was an indispensable party and was not
  in the case [22].
- **Texas and New Mexico v. Colorado (No. 29 Original), 1966.** Texas and New Mexico alleged that Colorado had under-delivered in every
  year 1952-1965 except 1958 [23]. Colorado blamed drought and the river's deterioration, and counterclaimed that New Mexico was itself
  more than 420,000 acre-feet short at the end of 1966 [23]. The United States joined. In 1968 the case was continued on Colorado's
  undertaking to deliver [23], and it was dismissed in 1985 [21, 23].

## A timeline of the era

- **1938** Caballo Dam begins storing water below Elephant Butte; winter power releases from Elephant Butte (plant from 1940) are held
  there for summer [10].
- **1942** the lake passes its spillway crest for the first time, at 4,409.15 feet against a crest of 4,407 (the water-year 1941
  maximum was 4,399.2) [6]; our storage record peaks at 2.30 million acre-feet on 16 June 1942.
- **1943-1950** practically normal supplies for eight years; carryover nearly gone by 1951 [13].
- **1947** Reclamation estimates middle-valley losses to riparian vegetation, wet sands and the river at more than 300,000 acre-feet a
  year [11]. Salt cedar has spread through the valley since 1935 [27].
- **1948-1950** Flood Control Acts authorize the Middle Rio Grande Project [2].
- **1951** the record dry year: the head of the reservoir has receded about 40 miles below San Marcial [2]. Texas sues New Mexico [22].
  New Mexico's Compact debit is 331,800 acre-feet, 131,200 over its cap [4]. Upstream storage is increased against Article VII [4]. Work
  on the conveyance channel begins [2].
- **1952** Colorado under-delivers about 154,000 acre-feet [3, 23]; its debit grows every year but 1958 to 1967 [23]. Project farmers
  turn to wells: pumping in the Elephant Butte Irrigation District, under 5,500 acre-feet in 1949, is about 290,000 by 1956 [24].
- **6 August 1954** the lake's lowest reading: 9,900 acre-feet (archive).
- **1956** the State Engineer declares the Rio Grande underground water basin and requires pumping's effect on the river to be offset;
  upheld in City of Albuquerque v. Reynolds (1962) [25].
- **1957** Texas v. New Mexico dismissed [22].
- **1953-1959** the conveyance channel opens in stages; channel works save an estimated 552,000 acre-feet between Cochiti and the lake in
  1951-1961 [2, 12].
- **1963** Abiquiu Dam closes on the Chama (flood and sediment control) [6].
- **1964** Colorado delivers 43% of what it owes [4]; the lake ends water year 1964 at 49,000 acre-feet (archive).
- **1966** Texas and New Mexico sue Colorado [23]. **1967** Colorado's debit peaks at 944,400 acre-feet [4].
- **1968** the case is continued on Colorado's undertaking to deliver; it does so every year through 1984 [3, 23].
- **1970-1971** Azotea Tunnel and Heron Dam: San Juan-Chama water begins entering the Chama [28].
- **By 1972** New Mexico's debit is cleared [2, 4].
- **1979-1980** Reclamation hands operation of Project works to the irrigation districts [24].
- **1980** capacity survey: 2,110,298 acre-feet at the crest, against 2,634,800 in 1915 [6].
- **March 1985** diversions into the conveyance channel stop after the rising lake buries its lower reach in sediment [2].
- **13 June 1985** the Commission declares a spill, counting upstream flood storage; all debits are cancelled [4, 21]. **December 1985**
  the case against Colorado is dismissed [21, 23].

## What is still open

1. **Why the Rio Chama fell** by 73,000 acre-feet a year or more against the 1930s while its mountains got the same precipitation. This
   is now the largest unexplained piece. Candidates not yet examined: Chama-valley irrigation, El Vado's operation and evaporation outside
   Article VII years, and the gauge itself.
2. **Why Colorado kept 79,000 acre-feet a year more in 1957-1967** than in the 1930s or after 1968. The court record names no cause.
   Groundwater development in the San Luis Valley is often suggested but we have not read a source that shows it.
3. **The headwater gauges are not natural flow.** Upstream reservoirs (four totalling 126,100 acre-feet by 1969, including Platoro,
   1951) and transmountain imports are not corrected here [4, 14].
4. **Storage on one capacity table.** The stored series appears to use the table in force at the time. Recomputing storage from
   elevation on a single table would make "lowest since 1956" exact.
5. **The water balance after 2000.** Storage change minus (inflow minus release) was 6.5-7.8 feet per acre of lake in every era to 1999.
   Since 2000 it has been only 2.7: either water reaches the lake that the San Marcial gauges miss, or a gauge or the area series is off
   at low levels.
6. **New Mexico's balances for 1952-1958**, and the Commission reports for those years.
7. **The standard histories have not been read for this report:** Phillips, Hall and Black, *Reining in the Rio Grande* (2011); Clark,
   *Water in New Mexico* (1987). A reviewer should check the answer against them before publication.

## Method and caveats

- **Matched years.** Every comparison of pieces uses only the years in which all of them have data; the number of years is shown.
- **Flows**: daily means from the archive, one copy per gauge and day; a water year needs 330 days.
- **Climate**: NOAA nClimDiv divisions 0505 (Colorado's Rio Grande basin) and 2902 (New Mexico's Northern Mountains). Winter is
  November-April. Early values are uncertain [18]. Snow courses from NRCS, April 1 survey, through the archive [30]. The tree-ring
  reconstruction is Woodhouse and others, read from NOAA's file; the period means are ours [15].
- **Advocates' documents.** The Sullivan [1] and Ferguson [24] declarations were filed for parties to Texas v. New Mexico and Colorado
  (No. 141 Original), and the No. 29 Original pleadings are each side's case [23]. We use their figures, not their characterizations.
  Ferguson calls the 1930s hydrologically "average to above average". USGS has the Rio Grande near Del Norte below its mean in 7 of the
  11 years 1930-1940 [13].
- **Agency papers** (Gorbach, Vandiver, Gaume) were written by engineers of Reclamation, Colorado and New Mexico: first-hand, not neutral.
- **Compact balances** were read by OCR from scanned reports. 1951, 1960, 1963 and 1981 were checked by eye, and the 1951 letter was
  read again for this version.
- **Our earlier errors, corrected here:**
  - Comparing only against 1916-1940.
  - Mixing years within a period.
  - A precipitation-matched comparison on 3 and 5 years.
  - Calling the headwater gauges nearly natural.
  - Treating the 1985 spill as physical.
  - Dating the first spill to 1941.
