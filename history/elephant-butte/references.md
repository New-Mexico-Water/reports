## Elephant Butte history (1950s-1980s)

Sources read for [reports/history/elephant-butte.md](reports/history/elephant-butte.md), 2026-10-11. Each was opened
and the passage used was read; the dated events are in `history/elephant-butte/events.yaml`.

### Sullivan 2023 (court declaration)

Sullivan, G. K. (2023). *Second Declaration of Gregory K. Sullivan, P.E., in support of joint motion of the State of
Texas, State of New Mexico, and State of Colorado for entry of consent decree supporting the Rio Grande Compact.*
Texas v. New Mexico and Colorado, No. 141 Original, Office of the Special Master, 3 February 2023.
<https://ecf.ca8.uscourts.gov/files/smDocuments/Exhibit%20G%2002032023%20Gregory%20Sullivan%20Declaration%20FINAL.pdf>

**Says.** Inflow to Elephant Butte at the San Marcial gage averaged 1,131,000 acre-feet a year in 1890-1937 and
568,000 in 1951-1978 (the "D2 period"), with three-year minimum averages of 441,000 and 205,000 (paragraph 9). Project
storage was enough until the Compact was signed in 1938, but the droughts of the 1950s, 1960s and 1970s showed it was
inadequate and farmers drilled supplemental wells, which Reclamation encouraged (paragraphs 9, 11-12).

**Does not say.** Why the inflow fell (the declaration is about the 2023 settlement, and it is a filing for the
settling states, so it is advocacy, not a neutral study). Our own gauges give 565,000 acre-feet for 1951-1978, which
supports its figure.

### Gorbach 1999

Gorbach, C. (1999). History and significance of the Low-Flow Conveyance Channel: what is its future? In *New Mexico
Water Resources Research Institute conference proceedings, 1999*.
<https://nmwrri.nmsu.edu/publications/water-conference-proceedings/wcp-documents/w44/gorbach.pdf>

**Says.** The early-1940s floods filled the reservoir and plugged the channels into it with sediment; by the record dry
year 1951 the head of the reservoir was about 40 miles below San Marcial and losses between San Marcial and the lake were
then estimated at more than 140,000 acre-feet a year. The channel was built from October 1951 (first part finished end of
1953, to San Acacia in 1959), carried most of the river in the 1960s-70s, and cut the San Acacia-San Marcial loss by about
40,000 acre-feet a year. New Mexico's Compact debit of more than 500,000 acre-feet (1943-1956) was gone by 1972. Low flow
lasted from the mid-1940s through the 1970s (San Marcial about two-thirds of its long-term average; about 40% in 1950-56);
flows rose from 1979, the reservoir filled in 1985 for the first time since 1942, and the spill cancelled all debits.
Diversions into the channel stopped in March 1985.

**Does not say.** Year-by-year figures behind its averages; the savings figures are the author's estimates. The author was
a Reclamation engineer writing about Reclamation's own project.

### Vandiver 1999

Vandiver, S. E. (1999). The administration of the Rio Grande Compact in Colorado. In *New Mexico Water Resources Research
Institute conference proceedings, 1999*. <https://nmwrri.nmsu.edu/publications/water-conference-proceedings/wcp-documents/w44/vandiver.pdf>

**Says.** Colorado under-delivered about 154,000 acre-feet in 1952, beginning under-deliveries until 1967, when its debit
reached about 940,000 acre-feet; Texas and New Mexico sued in 1966; under a May 1968 stipulation Colorado met or exceeded its
obligation every year 1968-1984, reducing the debit by about 430,000 acre-feet. **Does not say** why Colorado under-delivered
("largely unknown"). The author wrote as a Colorado water official.

### Orvis 1989 (sedimentation survey)

Orvis, C. J. (1989). *Elephant Butte Reservoir 1988 Sedimentation Survey.* U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, Denver Office.
<https://www.usbr.gov/tsc/techreferences/reservoir/Elephant%20Butte%20Reservoir%201988%20Sedimentation%20Survey.pdf>

**Says.** Capacity 2,065,010 acre-feet at elevation 4,407 ft in February 1988; 570,000 acre-feet of sediment trapped since 1915
(about 7,800 a year on average, 5,660 a year since 1980).

### Autobee 1994, Gaume 1999, Gahan 2013 (added in the second pass)

Autobee, R. (1994). *Rio Grande Project.* Bureau of Reclamation, Projects and Facilities history. <https://www.usbr.gov/projects/pdf.php?id=179>.
**Says** (p. 18-19, 22): water first went over the spillway in late June 1941; a drought from 1943 to 1952 with little snow in
the Rockies and a shortage of over a million acre-feet over nine years; farmers drilled 700 wells and planted low-water crops; by
1980 capacity had fallen from 2,638,000 to 2,110,298 acre-feet. **Does not say** how the shortage figure was computed. Written by
Reclamation about its own project.

Gaume, N. (1999). The Rio Grande Compact: It's the Law! New Mexico's obligations and compliance. In *NM WRRI conference proceedings,
1999*. <https://nmwrri.nmsu.edu/publications/water-conference-proceedings/wcp-documents/w44/gaume.pdf>. **Says**: Reclamation
concluded (1947) that riparian vegetation, wetted sands and the river were losing more than 300,000 acre-feet a year to
evapotranspiration; the State Engineer ruled in 1956 that groundwater pumping's effect on the river must be offset by retiring
surface uses; Texas brought Supreme Court litigation during the 1950s drought. The author was with New Mexico's Interstate Stream
Commission.

Gahan, A. H. (2013). *Middle Rio Grande Project.* Bureau of Reclamation, Historic Reclamation Projects.
<https://www.usbr.gov/history/ProjectHistories/Middle%20Rio%20Grande%20Project%20.pdf>. **Says**: Reclamation estimated 143,000
acre-feet a year lost in the aggraded, vegetated channel between the Bosque del Apache and the reservoir's Narrows; channel works
saved an estimated 71,000 acre-feet in 1961 and 552,000 over 1951-1961 between Cochiti and Elephant Butte; the Compact forbids
added upstream storage in post-1929 reservoirs while Elephant Butte holds less than 400,000 acre-feet of usable water; about
60,000 acres are irrigated in the Conservancy District.

Compact delivery schedules: the Compact Commission reports reprint the Article III tables (e.g. a Conejos index supply of 200,000
acre-feet requires 45,000 at the Conejos mouth; 700,000 requires 476,000), which is why Colorado's obligation falls faster than
its supply in dry years.
