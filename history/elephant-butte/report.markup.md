editor's notes are the boxed blocks marked **E1, E2, ...**. The revised text is in [report.edited.md](report.edited.md).

Severity:

- **MAJOR**: a wrong or unsupported claim that has to be fixed or withdrawn.
- **MINOR**: imprecise, incomplete or mislabelled.

Where a note says "archive", the number was recomputed from the nmwater archive with the report's own script. Where it cites a document,
that document was downloaded and the passage read. The new documents are listed at the end.

## The verdict in one paragraph

The report's headline cause does not survive its own archive. "Less snow" is measured against 1916-1940, which starts in the
wettest stretch of the Rio Grande's 500-year tree-ring record (1905-1930). Against the 1930s, the snow and the headwater flow were the same
as in the low era: on the 12 years when every gauge ran, the headwaters carried 795,000 acre-feet a year against 783,000, under 6.9 against
7.1 inches of winter precipitation. Yet Otowi ran 167,000 acre-feet lower and the lake held a third as much. The difference lies downstream
of the snow: more water kept in Colorado (an extra 54,000 to 79,000 acre-feet a year in 1957-1967, matching the Compact debit Colorado ran
up), a Rio Chama that ran about 73,000 lower even with imported water counted in it, smaller northern New Mexico tributaries, and a lake
that started the era empty because the Project kept delivering normal supplies for eight years after it filled in 1942. Three further
claims are contradicted by documents: that the reservoir "was run as before" (allotments fell; wells took over); that the lake "filled and
spilled" in 1985 (the Commission declared a spill "on paper"); and that water first went over the spillway in June 1941 (it did not until
1942). The report can be saved, but the answer has to be rewritten around those points.

# Why Elephant Butte was low from the 1950s to about 1980

An investigation of Elephant Butte Reservoir's low era (about 1946-1985), checked against this archive. Second version,
2026-10-11: it replaces a first pass that named the actors but could not explain most of the shortfall. Nothing here is
published. The per-year numbers are in [elephant-butte.csv](elephant-butte.csv) and the period averages in
[elephant-butte-periods.csv](elephant-butte-periods.csv), both regenerated by `scripts/elephant_butte.py`. Dated events are
in `history/elephant-butte/events.yaml`; full citations, with what each source does and does not say, in
[references.md](references.md). Numbers in brackets refer to the sources at the end.

## The answer

The lake was low for about thirty years because the river that feeds it carried far less water, and the shortfall grew at
every step on the way down. It was not because the reservoir was run differently, and not mainly because of rain in New
Mexico.

> **E1 — FATAL. The baseline is the wettest stretch in 500 years; against the 1930s the snow was the same.**
> Every comparison in the report is against 1916-1940 (or 1916-1950). That period starts inside 1905-1930, the highest 26-year mean in the
> Rio Grande near Del Norte tree-ring reconstruction, 1508-2002 (Woodhouse and others; NOAA Paleoclimatology file `riogrande2013flow.txt`:
> 737,000 acre-feet a year in 1905-1930 against a 500-year mean of 644,000 and 623,000 in 1957-1978). USGS Professional Paper 372-D (p. D4)
> calls 1905-1929 "a wetter quarter of a century". The archive splits the baseline sharply: 1916-1926 headwaters 1,128,000 af under 9.0 in of
> winter precipitation; 1927-1940 854,000 under 6.9 in. On the 12 years of 1927-1940 when every gauge ran, the headwaters carried 795,000 and
> the low era 783,000 (-1.5%); winter precipitation was 6.9 against 7.1 in, a little *higher* in the low era. Tree rings agree (Del Norte
> reconstructed 631,000 for 1927-1940 against 623,000). An expert would put the 1930s next to the 1960s and ask why the lake held 1,064,000 af
> on average in one and 369,000 in the other with the same snow. The report never asks. **Fix:** compare with the 1930s as well; state that by
> the 500-year record 1957-1978 was only about 3% below normal at Del Norte, and that the wet 1905-1930 and 1979-1999 periods are the
> anomalies.


1. **Less snow in the mountains.** Winter (November to April) precipitation over Colorado's Rio Grande headwaters averaged
   7.1 inches in 1957-1978 against 7.9 in 1916-1950, and over New Mexico's northern mountains 5.0 against 5.7: 10-12% less.
   Summer monsoon rain was about the same or a little higher, so yearly totals looked close to normal. But the Rio Grande is
   a snowmelt river: summer rain adds little to it. Spring was not warmer (5.3 °C against 5.5). The headwaters at Del Norte
   and on the Conejos, where the flow is nearly natural, carried 783,000 acre-feet a year against 974,000 before 1941 (-20%).
   Reclamation's history of the Project describes the same thing for 1943-1952: little snow in the Rockies, minuscule runoff [10].

> **E5 — MAJOR. Baselines switch, and one claim reverses with them.** Winter precipitation is compared with 1916-1950 (7.9 in) but flows
> with 1916-1940. Against 1916-1940 the low era's winter deficit is 7.7 to 7.1 (-8%), not 10-12%. "Summer monsoon rain was about the same or a
> little higher" is true only against 1916-1950 (5.75 in); against 1916-1940 it was lower (6.1 to 5.9). One baseline, stated once.

> **E12 — MAJOR. "Spring was not warmer (5.3 °C against 5.5)" leans on data that cannot resolve 0.2 °C.** Vose and others (2014),
> the nClimDiv documentation, say station density in the West falls going back, divisional precipitation error shrinks until about 1950,
> and early area-mean temperature errors can exceed 1 °C. Mountain winter precipitation before the 1980s also rests on few high-elevation
> gauges. A 10% winter-precipitation difference between 1916-1940 and 1957-1978 is inside that uncertainty. **Fix:** say so, and lean on
> the gauges, the snow courses (from 1937) and the tree rings, which agree with each other.

> **E6 — MAJOR. "Where the flow is nearly natural."** The Compact itself corrects the Rio Grande near Del Norte for the operation of
> reservoirs built after 1937, and its Conejos index adds Los Pinos and San Antonio rivers for April-October (Compact, Article III, reprinted in
> the Commission's 1951 report). The Commission's own gauge note (1969 report) says Del Norte is regulated by four reservoirs totalling 126,100 af
> and fed by six transmountain diversions; Platoro (1951, about 60,000 af) is above the Mogote gauge. The effects may be small (PP 372-B,
> p. B13), but "nearly natural" is the wrong word and the report's sum is not the Compact's index supply. **Fix:** "the least-used gauges
> above the San Luis Valley; not corrected for upstream reservoirs or imports".

2. **Colorado took a larger share of a smaller river.** The Rio Grande Compact sets Colorado's delivery as a share of its
   headwater supply that falls faster than the supply: at a Conejos supply of 200,000 acre-feet Colorado owes only 45,000 at
   its mouth [4]. On top of that, Colorado under-delivered from 1952 [3]: in 1964 it delivered 57,600 acre-feet, 43% of what
   it owed [4], and its accrued debit reached 944,400 acre-feet in 1967 [4]. San Luis Valley use rose by about 41,000
   acre-feet a year. The flow crossing into New Mexico at Lobatos fell from 489,000 to 256,000 acre-feet a year (-48%).

> **E7 — MAJOR. The Compact mechanics are misdescribed.** "At a Conejos supply of 200,000 acre-feet Colorado owes only 45,000 at its mouth"
> is the Conejos schedule alone; Colorado's obligation at Lobatos is the Conejos schedule value plus the Del Norte schedule value, less 10,000
> af (Article III). The report also leaves out New Mexico's side, which works the same way: since the Commission's 1948 resolution New
> Mexico's delivery is measured as Elephant Butte effective index supply (release plus storage change), and the schedule lets New Mexico
> consume a larger share of a small Otowi flow than of a large one (Gaume 1999). Lake evaporation is charged to New Mexico. So the Compact
> "amplifies" a dry year at both state lines, and the report should say so for both. "San Luis Valley use rose by about 41,000" also needs its
> split: the archive gives 568,000 af a year kept in Colorado in 1957-1967 against 489,000 in 1928-1940 and 486,000 in 1968-1978, after the
> stipulation. That pattern, not the 1957-1978 average, is the evidence.

3. **New Mexico's tributaries and the Rio Chama were down too**, by about 38,000 and 79,000 acre-feet a year, the same
   direction as the snow. The river at Otowi fell from 1,211,000 to 847,000 acre-feet a year (-30%).

> **E10 — MAJOR. The imported water is in the low-era Chama figure, so the native decline is larger.** San Juan-Chama water entered the Chama
> through Azotea Tunnel from late 1970 (Glaser, Reclamation project history): about 672,000 af in 1971-1978 (USGS 08284160, 84,000 a year),
> or roughly 30,000 a year averaged over 1957-1978, some of it held in Heron. The method note says the comparison is "little affected"; the
> effect runs the wrong way for the report's argument: the native Chama fell by more than 79,000 (or 73,000 against the 1930s). "The same
> direction as the snow" is not an explanation: New Mexico's northern-mountain winter precipitation was 5.0 in in both 1927-1940 and
> 1957-1978, yet the Chama fell. El Vado (1935) cannot be the reason during the low lake, because Article VII barred storing native water
> while Project storage was under 400,000 af (Gahan 2013). **Fix:** report the Chama decline as the largest unexplained piece.

4. **The middle valley's losses stayed about the same in acre-feet**, so they took a larger share. Between Otowi and San
   Marcial about 192,000-261,000 acre-feet a year never reached the lake in every period. Reclamation found in 1947 that
   riparian vegetation, wet sands and the river were losing more than 300,000 acre-feet a year [11], and the silted,
   overgrown channel just above the lake lost about 143,000 more [12]. Reclamation's channel works and the Low-Flow Conveyance
   Channel won back an estimated 40,000-71,000 acre-feet a year from the 1950s [2, 12]. Inflow to the lake at San Marcial
   averaged 565,000 acre-feet a year in 1951-1978, against 948,000-1,011,000 before 1951; a court declaration from Reclamation's
   records gives 568,000 against 1,131,000 [1].

> **E11 — MAJOR. Reclamation's "more than 300,000" is gross; our 192,000-261,000 is net of tributaries.** As written, the reader sees
> Reclamation's loss alone exceed ours, plus "143,000 more". Reconcile: the Jemez, Rio Puerco and Rio Salado added roughly 80,000-90,000 af a
> year in 1957-1978 (archive: 42,000, 30,000, 12,000), and Albuquerque's wastewater added more as its pumping grew. Net 219,000 plus those is
> a gross depletion of roughly 300,000-350,000, consistent with Reclamation's estimate. Say whether the 143,000 is part of it (it is a reach
> of the same valley). Also missing: salt cedar spread "significantly" in area and density 1935-1947 and gained more than 3,000 acres between
> Bernardo and San Marcial in 1947-1955 (Reclamation, 1957 sedimentation report, pp. 25, 40); Albuquerque's pumping took about 13,000 af a
> year from the river by 1960 and 38,000 by 1979 (Kernodle and others 1995, USGS WRIR 94-4251). Losses "stayed about the same" only because
> the channel works offset rising depletion; say so.

5. **The reservoir was run as before.** It released 89% of its inflow in 1957-1978, the same share as in 1916-1940, so with
   half the inflow it held about a third as much. Releases stayed well under the Compact's "normal" 790,000 acre-feet (470,900
   in 1951; 57-65% of normal in the 1960s) [4]. Farmers adapted: about 700 wells drilled during the 1943-1952 drought and
   low-water crops [10]; supplemental pumping that kept the Project going through the 1950s-70s [1].

> **E3 — FATAL. "The reservoir was run as before" is contradicted.** (a) The low era began with an empty lake, and the report never asks
> how it emptied. USGS PP 372-D (p. D18): after filling in 1942 the reservoir gave practically normal supplies for 8 consecutive years, though
> inflow was under half the average in 4 of them, and by 1951 nearly all carryover storage had been used. Archive: 1943-1950 releases averaged
> 789,000 af against inflow of 669,000; storage fell from 1,931,000 (end of water year 1942) to 333,000 (1950) and 19,000 (1951). (b) After
> 1951 operations changed: the Ferguson declaration (No. 141 Original, filed for the United States) says Project releases gave at least
> 3.0 acre-feet per acre every year 1930-1939, but the full 3.0 in only 10 of the 28 years 1951-1978; groundwater pumping in the Elephant
> Butte Irrigation District rose from under 5,500 af (1949) to about 290,000 (1956). PP 372-D calls the farmers' wells "a new reservoir".
> The 89% release ratio is a ratio of two flows; it says nothing about allotments, carryover or the wells. **Fix:** replace the claim with
> the drawdown and the change in supply. Ferguson is an advocate's filing (for the United States): it calls the 1930s "average to above
> average" hydrologically (para. 13), where PP 372-D (p. D4) has the Rio Grande near Del Norte below its mean in 7 of the 11 years 1930-1940
> and the tree rings put 1927-1940 slightly below the 500-year mean. Use its allotment and pumping figures, not its characterisation of the climate.

> **E14 — MINOR. "With half the inflow it held about a third as much."** For 1957-1978 inflow was two thirds of 1916-1940 (628,000 against
> 948,000), not half. Half is the 1951-1978 against 1890-1937 figure from Sullivan.

> **E17 — MINOR. Mexico.** Releases include the 60,000 af a year owed to Mexico under the 1906 Convention, which is cut in drought in proportion
> to deliveries to Project lands; deliveries were under 60,000 in 1947, 1951 (43,000) and 1952 (49,700) (Special Master's report in No. 29
> Original). Say so wherever "released" is used.

6. **It ended with snow.** In 1979-1999 winter precipitation over the headwaters was 8.6 inches, the highest of any period, the
   headwaters ran at 946,000 acre-feet and Otowi at 1,276,000. Colorado had delivered in full since a 1968 court stipulation [3].
   The lake filled and spilled in 1985, and the spill cancelled every Compact debit [2, 4].

> **E9 — MAJOR. 1985 was a spill declared on paper.** Colorado's State Engineer, 1985 annual report: Elephant Butte "spilled on paper" on
> 13 June 1985, because the Compact Commission had approved flood storage in upstream New Mexico reservoirs to avoid an uncontrolled spill
> and gave spill credit for that water; the spill erased 605,000 af of Colorado's debit and 115,000 of New Mexico's. The Commission's 1985
> report records the finding "pursuant to the May 13, 1985 agreement of the Commissioners", 313,600 af gained in New Mexico's upstream storage,
> and only about 7,800 af physically spilled, in July. **Fix:** say what happened; it is more interesting than "the lake filled and spilled".


**Today** looks like the low era for the same first reason (winter precipitation 7.3 inches over Colorado's headwaters since 2000)
with a new one added: spring is about 1.2 °C warmer (6.7 °C against 5.3-5.5). Warmth is a known way for the same snow to yield
less river, but we have not read a study that measures it for this river. Inflow since 2000 has averaged 492,000 acre-feet, below the low era, and on 29 July 2026 the lake held 26,732 acre-feet,
its lowest since 1956.

> **E19 — MINOR. "We have not read a study that measures it for this river"** is no longer true: Lehner and others (2017, Geophysical
> Research Letters) reconstruct the upper Rio Grande's runoff efficiency to 1571 and find the decline since the 1980s unprecedented, with
> warm temperatures making very low runoff years 2.5-3 times as likely in dry years. They also find the 1980s the highest-flow decade of
> the reconstruction, so the recovery the report credits to snow is itself one of the rarest events in 445 years.

> **E18 — MINOR. "Lowest since 1956" depends on the capacity table.** The daily record confirms it (nothing below 26,732 af between October
> 1956 and July 2026). But Elephant Butte's capacity at the crest fell from 2,634,800 af (1915) to 2,010,900 (2017 survey), the dead pool
> was full of sediment by the 1960s, and the stored series appears to use the table in force at the time (its 1942 peak of 2.30 million
> exceeds today's crest capacity). The same acre-feet now sit at a different elevation and area. Add the caveat; recomputing storage
> from elevation on one table is listed as work to do.

Water owed and not delivered is water the lake did not get. Under the Compact, while the lake held less than 400,000 acre-feet
of usable water, Colorado and New Mexico could not add storage in reservoirs built after 1929 [12]; in 1951 the Commission recorded
that upstream storage was increased anyway, against Article VII [4]. Texas sued New Mexico during the 1950s drought [11], and
Texas and New Mexico sued Colorado in 1966 [3].

> **E8 — MAJOR. "Water owed and not delivered is water the lake did not get."** Not one for one. Colorado's shortfall is measured at Lobatos,
> and under Article IV New Mexico may consume part of any extra water passing Otowi; the middle valley would have lost part of the rest.
> New Mexico's own debit is measured at the lake (effective index supply), so it is water the lake did not get, by definition. **Fix:**
> "Water Colorado owed and did not deliver at the state line was water that could not reach the lake; some of it would have been used or lost
> in New Mexico on the way." Also state the Article VI caps (Colorado 100,000 af, New Mexico 200,000 af): Colorado's debit was pleaded as a
> breach of its cap from 1953 (complaint, No. 29 Original), not just a large number.

> **E15 — MINOR. Texas v. New Mexico, No. 9 Original.** Texas filed in 1951 against New Mexico and the Middle Rio Grande Conservancy
> District, asking the Court to stop storage in El Vado and other reservoirs until New Mexico's debit fell to 200,000 af. The Court dismissed
> it in 1957 (352 U.S. 991) because the United States, trustee for the Pueblos' prior rights, was an indispensable party and absent (Special
> Master's reports and the Solicitor General's memorandum, printed in No. 29 Original). The report gives neither the relief sought nor the
> outcome. In No. 29 Original Colorado blamed drought and the "physical deterioration" of the river, and counterclaimed that New Mexico was
> itself more than 420,000 af short at the end of 1966: the report quotes only the complaint side.


## A timeline of the era

- **June 1941** water goes over the spillway for the first time; the lake went from a near-record low to a record high in six
  months [10]. Our storage record peaks on 16 June 1942 at 2.30 million acre-feet; sources differ on whether the last spill
  before 1985 was 1941 or 1942.

> **E13 — MAJOR. No June 1941 spill.** Orvis (1989), the 1988 sedimentation survey, gives the water-year 1941 maximum stage as 4,399.2 ft and
> 1942 as 4,409.15 ft, against a spillway crest of 4,407 ft. The report cites Autobee (1994) for June 1941 and then hedges; the survey settles
> it: the first spill was in 1942. The original capacity is also 2,634,800 af in Orvis's table, not 2,638,000.

- **1943-1952** drought: little snow in the Rockies, a shortage of over a million acre-feet over nine years; farmers drill about
  700 wells and plant low-water crops [10].
- **1943-1956** New Mexico runs a Compact debit of more than 500,000 acre-feet [2]; by the end of 1951 it was 131,200 over the
  Compact's limit [4].
- **1947** Reclamation finds riparian vegetation, wet sands and the river losing more than 300,000 acre-feet a year in the middle
  valley [11].
- **1948-1950** Flood Control Acts authorize the Middle Rio Grande Project (dams, channel works) [2].
- **1951** the record dry year: the head of the reservoir has receded about 40 miles below San Marcial; the river below the Bosque
  del Apache is disconnected pools [2]. Work on the Low-Flow Conveyance Channel begins in October [2]. Upstream storage is
  increased against Article VII [4].
- **1952** Colorado under-delivers about 154,000 acre-feet, "reasons largely unknown", and keeps falling behind until 1967 [3].
- **6 August 1954** the lake's lowest reading: 9,900 acre-feet (archive).
- **1956** the State Engineer rules that groundwater pumping's effect on the river must be offset by retiring surface water rights [11].

> **E16 — MINOR.** In 1956 the State Engineer declared the Rio Grande underground water basin and applied the offset rule; the courts upheld
> it in City of Albuquerque v. Reynolds, 71 N.M. 428 (1962). Give both dates.

- **1953-1959** the conveyance channel opens in stages, reaching San Acacia in 1959; in the 1960s-70s it carries most of the river
  and cuts losses by about 40,000 acre-feet a year [2]. Channel works save an estimated 552,000 acre-feet between Cochiti and the
  lake over 1951-1961 [12].
- **1964** Colorado delivers 43% of what it owes [4]; the lake ends water year 1964 at 49,000 acre-feet (archive).
- **1966-1968** Texas and New Mexico sue Colorado; from 1968 Colorado delivers in full under a stipulation [3].
- **1967** Colorado's debit peaks at 944,400 acre-feet [4].
- **1971** Heron Dam completed; imported San Juan-Chama water is stored there for Albuquerque, the Conservancy District and others,
  not for Elephant Butte [7].
- **By 1972** New Mexico's debit is cleared [2, 4].
- **1979** the river turns high [2]; winter precipitation over the headwaters averages 8.6 inches for the next two decades.
- **By 1980** silt has cut the lake's capacity from 2,638,000 acre-feet to 2,110,298, according to Reclamation's history [10].
- **March 1985** diversions into the conveyance channel stop after the rising lake buried its outlet in sediment [2].
- **1985** the lake fills and spills; all Compact debits are cancelled [2, 4]; the case against Colorado is dismissed in December [3].

> **E9 (cont.)** Same correction in the timeline. Also: the case against Colorado was dismissed with prejudice on 9 December 1985
> (Colorado State Engineer 1985; stipulated motion, No. 29 Original), and the dismissal expressly did not decide Colorado's defences.


## What is still open

1. **How much of the snow shortfall is climate and how much is upstream use above the "natural" gauges.** Del Norte and the Conejos
   are the Compact's index gauges and treated as natural, but reservoirs and diversions above them (e.g. Platoro, 1951) are not
   corrected here.
2. **Why the lake's water balance changed after 2000.** Storage change minus (inflow minus release), mostly evaporation, was 6.5-7.8
   feet of water per acre of lake in every era to 1999 but only 2.7 since 2000: either water reaches the lake that the San Marcial
   gauges miss, or a gauge or the lake-area series is off at low levels.
3. **Why the middle-valley loss has grown since 2000** (308,000 acre-feet a year). San Juan-Chama water and Albuquerque's effluent
   have added to the river since the 1970s, which raises Otowi and may raise losses; not researched here.
4. **The Compact reports for 1952-1958** are not online, so New Mexico's and Colorado's balances in the worst drought years rest on
   Gorbach and Vandiver.

## Method and caveats

- **Flows**: daily means from the archive, one copy per gauge and day (`river_flow.PRIORITY`); a water year needs 330 days. Inflow at
  San Marcial is USGS 08358500 (the whole river, kept to 1964 as the total of the floodway and the conveyance channel) and after that
  the floodway (08358400) plus the channel (08358300). An earlier version used the floodway alone in 1951-1952 and missed 282,000
  acre-feet in 1952.
- **Comparisons**: the first version compared years of "normal" New Mexico rain, but those 1957-1978 years were about 7% drier than the
  pre-1950 years they were compared with. Matching on precipitation over Colorado's headwaters instead gives the 19% figure above.
- **Climate**: NOAA nClimDiv monthly precipitation, temperature and Palmer index by climate division (divisions are large; their
  averages include valleys).
- **San Juan-Chama** water has been added to the Rio Chama since the early 1970s, so post-1971 Otowi and Chamita flows include some
  imported water; the low-era comparison (to 1978) is little affected.
- **Compact balances** were read by OCR from scanned reports; 1951, 1960, 1963 and 1981 were checked by eye, 1970 rests on a research
  agent's reading. **Allotments** are from a USGS data release.
- **Sources** include a court declaration filed for the settling states and conference papers by agency engineers (Reclamation,
  Colorado's water division, New Mexico's Interstate Stream Commission): first-hand but not neutral. Research agents found the
  documents; every source a conclusion rests on was opened and the passage read, except those marked otherwise in the events file.

> **E20 — MINOR. What an expert will ask about that the report does not mention.** The 1979-1980 handover of operations to the districts and
> the D1/D2 allocation curves (Joint Stipulated Facts, No. 141); the standard histories (Phillips, Hall and Black, *Reining in the Rio Grande*,
> 2011; Clark, *Water in New Mexico*, 1987), not read here and so not cited, but a historian will expect them; Pueblo prior and paramount
> rights, which shaped the 1950s litigation; the Compact reports for 1952-1958 (Colorado's balances for those years are in the No. 29
> complaint: 153,300 af at the end of 1952 rising to 506,200 in 1957). Also, the Sullivan and Ferguson declarations were filed for parties
> to No. 141 Original; say for whom.
